# Audit: car and aircraft models, performance, architecture

Date: 2026-11-19. Measured in the dev build (Chromium in the Claude desktop app, Mac, device pixel ratio 2, canvas 722 x 713 CSS px).

## 1. Car models

### What they are

The three cars (saloon, off-roader, supercar) are generated by Python scripts in Blender (`art/`): a control cage of cross-sections, a subdivision surface, boolean wheel arches, then panels cut by material, with gap and flange panels. Nothing is modelled by hand.

| Car | Triangles | Meshes in game | Shadow casters | Deformable meshes | Materials | GLB size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Saloon | 149k | 108 | 27 | 84 | 31 | 1.13 MB |
| Off-roader | 144k | 106 | 26 | 82 | 29 | 0.96 MB |
| Supercar | 163k | 97 | 24 | 73 | 29 | 1.39 MB |

### What is wrong with them

Looked at from three-quarter views in the game:

- **Shapes are soft and generic.** A subdivided cage gives rounded, inflated volumes. There are no crisp feature lines, no real surface transitions, and the cars do not read as a specific type (the saloon is not an Evo-style rally saloon).
- **Floating and detached details.** Mirrors hover off the doors, the supercar's wing sits on thin posts in the air, the saloon's A-pillar trim reads as a blue bar across the windscreen.
- **Holes and see-through areas.** The supercar's side intakes and the saloon's grille show through to the inside or end in visible edges.
- **Proportions.** The saloon's tail is long and heavy, the off-roader's nose is flat and box-like without detail, the wheels are a little small for their arches.
- **Materials.** 29 to 31 materials per car split every part into several meshes: about 100 draw calls per car.
- **Cost.** 145k to 163k triangles per car is a lot for what is on screen, and every triangle of the deformable panels is re-skinned by the crash deformation.

Conclusion: the procedural pipeline was a good way to get cars with correct part structure for crashes, but it does not produce good-looking cars. Better results come from a hand-made model, reworked into our part structure.

### Done

The three cars were replaced by hand-made CC BY 4.0 models (section 4), reworked by `art/import_car.py`:

| Car | Model | Triangles | Meshes in game | GLB |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rally saloon | Thunderbolt '96 | 18.8k (was 149k) | 43 (was 108) | 1.48 MB |
| Off-roader | Bokaroo '67 | 28.2k (was 144k) | 35 (was 106) | 1.39 MB |
| Supercar | V12 Goblin | 65.7k (was 163k) | 30 (was 97) | 1.45 MB |

Draw calls with the three cars in view: 276 in the city (was 443), 341 in the test area (was 554). Every crash, damage, driving and handling test passes with the new cars, after a front-stiffness calibration per car (`crush[0].force`, `elastic`).

### Original plan

1. Take freely licensed models made by people (licence must allow redistribution in a public repo: CC0 or CC-BY). Candidates are listed in section 4.
2. Rework each one in Blender: split into the parts the crash system needs (body, bonnet, boot, four doors, bumpers, glass panes, lights, mirrors, wheels), set hinge pivots, remove logos, reduce materials to about 8 (paint, glass, chrome, black trim, rubber, rim, lights, interior), aim for 40k to 80k triangles.
3. Keep the crash physics the same: crush rays are measured on the new shells, the deformation lattice binds to the new meshes.

## 2. Performance

### Measured

| | Test area | City | Grand Prix circuit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Render (CPU + GPU, synced) | 9.5 ms | 5.8 ms | 5.8 ms |
| One physics step, all cars and props | 0.24 ms | 0.23 ms | 0.23 ms |
| Draw calls (with the shadow pass) | 554 | 443 | 484 |

In the city, hiding the three cars takes the render from 5.8 ms to 2.4 ms; hiding the whole map only to 4.9 ms. **The cars are the main rendering cost**, because of their draw calls (about 100 each, plus the shadow pass) and their triangle count.

Halving the resolution only saved 1.2 ms on this machine (draw-call bound), but on a bigger window or a weaker GPU the pixel count dominates: at device pixel ratio 2 a full-screen 1440 x 900 window renders 5.2 million pixels.

### Findings

| # | Problem | Impact | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | Pixel ratio up to 2 on high-DPI screens | Up to 4x the pixel work | Fixed: capped at 1.5 and adaptive (down to 0.6 when frames run long); garage setting to pin it |
| P2 | About 100 draw calls and 150k triangles per car, 3 cars always drawn at full detail | Main render cost | Fixed: new models, 30 to 43 meshes and 19k to 66k triangles per car; draw calls down by about 40 % |
| P3 | Normals of every dented mesh recomputed in one go | About 40 ms stall on a big crash frame | Fixed: vertex budget per frame, round-robin |
| P4 | `all()` built a new array 3 times per physics step | Garbage-collector pauses | Fixed: one stable array |
| P5 | Street furniture sync built a `Set` every frame | Garbage | Fixed |
| P6 | Lap panel text rewritten every frame | Layout work | Fixed: 20 Hz |
| P7 | Each car has its own particle pools (sparks, smoke, tyre smoke, skids) | 12 draw calls and buffers for 3 cars | Acceptable; shared pool is a later step |
| P8 | Physics (0.23 ms per 120 Hz step) | Fine | - |

## 3. Architecture

| # | Problem | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | `main.ts` held everything: boot, garage wiring, frame loop, lap logic, teleports, debug API | Fixed: `main.ts` only builds systems; `Game` owns the loop, cars and actions; `RaceSystem` owns lap timing |
| A2 | Ground surface was a module-level global set by the map | Fixed: injected through the physics world (`physics.surfaceAt`) |
| A3 | Vehicle code imported the world module (surface grip) | Fixed: surface types and grip live in `core/physics` |
| A4 | `Vehicle.ts` is 850 lines (steering, assists, drivetrain, suspension, tyres in one step function) | Partly fixed: the steering and its aids are a separate `SteeringController`; the per-step Ackermann array allocation is gone. Drivetrain and tyre steps still to split |
| A5 | Car configs are JSON cast to the type without checks | Fixed: `tests/config.test.ts` validates every car |

Layering after the changes: `core` (math, physics, input, loop) <- `vehicle` and `damage` (simulation, no rendering) <- `render`, `world`, `ui` <- `game` (orchestration) <- `main` (boot).

Tests: 64 headless tests (driving, crashes, damage, handling, garage, map, lap timer, kerbs) run in about 20 s.

## 5. Second pass: loading, smoothness, robustness (2026-11-20)

Measured on the production build (`vite preview`, frames driven at 60 Hz with a GPU sync), before -> after:

| | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First frame after the loading screen | 311 ms | 45-59 ms (shaders and textures prepared under the loading screen) |
| Car files before the first frame | 3 (4.0 MB), after physics init | 2 (2.6 MB), in parallel with physics init; the third loads in the background |
| Worst frame of a warm 70 km/h wall crash | 21-24 ms (saloon), 26 ms (4x4) | 10-11 ms (saloon), 13 ms (4x4), 9.5-10.5 ms (supercar) |
| First frame after switching to a car loaded later | 140 ms | 37 ms |
| Idle frame, p50 / CPU | 3.2-3.7 ms / 1.2 ms | unchanged |

What changed:

- **Crash cost**: `Deformer.imprint` handles each lattice node once per obstacle per step (it was redone by every contact: 21 contacts and about 20,000 node updates per step on the 4x4); `updateMeshes` uses precomputed wear noise and inverse matrices, skips unchanged vertices and no longer allocates. The crush cap of each node is read at the node itself, from the crush rays that have actually crushed.
- **Loading**: physics and car downloads in parallel, byte progress, cars not on screen loaded after the first frame; `render/warmup.ts` compiles shaders against the game scene and uploads textures, at boot and for cars loaded later.
- **Robustness**: a watchdog resets a car whose state goes non-finite; a full-screen error with Reload for a failed boot or a lost WebGL context; the audio ignores non-finite input; an exception in the physics step pauses the game and shows the error instead of failing every frame; the garage car menu goes back to the car actually driven when a switch fails.
- **Architecture**: ESLint with enforced layering (`core <- vehicle, damage <- render, world, audio, debug <- game <- ui <- main`; type-only imports allowed only from damage to render and from game to ui), lint in CI; `Vehicle.fixedUpdate` split into 12 named phases with one reused step object; `WheelState` and tyre helpers in their own modules; boot and per-frame timings (`window.game.boot`, `frameStats`, debug overlay).
- **Checks**: 68 tests, lint, types, build; gitleaks (history and tree), npm audit (production) and Semgrep: nothing found.

Not done: Rapier without the base64-inlined WASM (`@dimforge/rapier3d` would save about 0.6 MB gzipped but needs a WASM plugin and top-level await in the build and tests); the very first crash of a session still costs one frame of 19-27 ms while the JavaScript engine optimises the crash code (JIT warm-up).

## 6. Open world (2026-11-20)

The world grew from a flat 2 km square to a 4 km terrain with relief, 13 km of country roads, seven places, about 50,000 trees and a lake. Measured on the production build like section 5 (frames driven at 60 Hz with a GPU sync, pixel ratio 1.5, the Mac's GPU).

| | Flat 2 km world | Open world |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frame time while driving (GPU bound) | 3.2-3.7 ms | 6.5-8 ms depending on the place |
| CPU per frame | 1.2-1.3 ms | 2.7-3.5 ms |
| Draw calls | 276-341 | 250-450 |
| Boot, scripts to first frame | about 0.6 s | about 0.9 s |

How the cost is kept down:

- **Terrain**: one height texture, four shared chunk grids (4, 8, 16, 32 m) displaced in the vertex shader; 64 chunks of 512 m, each culled and given a level of detail by distance; skirts hide the seams. The physics uses the same heights as a Rapier heightfield with the same triangle split (checked by a test to 1 cm).
- **Generation in a worker**: terrain, road profiles and carving, ground cover, tree positions and the map picture (about 300 ms of work) run in a Web Worker while the physics engine and the cars load; the main thread then spends about 100 ms building meshes and colliders. Large noise is sampled on coarse grids.
- **Trees**: the first version drew 85,000 trees with non-indexed models and cost about 4 ms of GPU time (3.6 million vertices per frame, whatever the resolution). Now: about 50,000 trees, indexed flat-shaded models, far models in 512 m blocks culled like any mesh, detailed models only within 170 m (refilled as the camera moves), shadows only from trees within 70 m. Trees now cost about 0.7 ms.
- **Shadow casters**: merged meshes that spanned the whole world (all the places in one batch, the whole city in one mesh per material) were drawn into the shadow map from anywhere. Places now have one batch each, city buildings are merged per district (2 x 3 blocks), and the city's small things (trees, rooftop clutter, street furniture) stop drawing beyond 700 m.
- **Sky**: follows the camera and draws after the opaque scenery, on the far plane, so its scattering shader only runs where nothing else covers the screen.
- **Colliders**: trees get colliders from a pool of 320 that follows the player; roads are trimeshes in 240 m pieces with internal-edge fixing; bridges and guard rails are boxes.

Remaining costs, in order: terrain fragment shading (about 1.4 ms), shadows (receiving and casting, about 1.5 ms), the cars themselves. Not done: streaming the world in and out, impostors for far trees, a texture atlas to merge the city's facade materials (it would save about 40 draw calls in the city).

A light aircraft to fly from the airfield, sourced like the cars: a hand-made model with a licence that allows redistribution, reworked in Blender into the parts the flight and damage models need.

### Candidates

Searched Sketchfab for low-wing, tricycle-gear four-seaters (the easiest to take off and land with a keyboard) under CC BY 4.0, with a complete exterior, a cockpit, separate or separable control surfaces and 20k to 80k triangles.

| Model | Author | Licence | Triangles | For | Against |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Piper PA-28 Cadet](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/piper-pa-28-cadet-ba310f1e1ba349c7a54ab9db92e48970) | helijah | CC BY 4.0 | 55k | The classic trainer, well documented | Older shape |
| [Diamond DA40](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/diamond-da40-dfda5fad07c24c12be13a23562fe83e3) | helijah | CC BY 4.0 | 72k | Modern, clean lines | Heavier |
| **[Cirrus SR22](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/cirrus-sr22-cba602c99c524cd4b40e5c2e5f9c5b4f)** (picked by the user) | KOG_THORNS | CC BY 4.0 as uploaded (see below) | 58k | Most liked, modern, full cockpit with a panel | Less documented upload |
| [Piper PA-18 Cub](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/413e3f81b0db4e0ebba0f27490732770) | not recorded | CC BY 4.0 | 18k | Light | High wing and a tail wheel: much harder to take off and land |

**Licence check.** The SR22's texture is signed "Cirrus SR22 : BARANGER Emmanuel 2013". Emmanuel Baranger (Helijah) made the Cirrus SR22 for the FlightGear simulator, distributed under the GNU GPL v2 ([FGMEMBERS/Cirrus-SR22](https://github.com/FGMEMBERS/Cirrus-SR22)). The Sketchfab upload is very probably a conversion of it, so the model is credited to him as well (`CREDITS.md`), his signature stays in the texture, and the source file, the Blender scene and the game model are all in this repository, which is what the GPL v2 asks of a modified version. If that is not acceptable, the PA-28 and DA40 above are uploads by Helijah himself under CC BY 4.0.

### What the source is

One 4.1 MB GLB, 57.9k triangles in 19 meshes with 18 materials and 21 textures (1024 px for the paint and the propeller, small ones for the panel and instruments), built facing +X at about 1:1 scale, every part of the exterior in a few big objects: the wings, flaps and ailerons are one mesh with the fuselage and the gear; the rudder and elevator are part of the tail. The texture carries a registration (BR-CS2), the maker's name and model logos.

### What the import does (`art/import_plane.py`, mapping in `art/imports/plane.json`)

- Orients and scales to the real aircraft (7.92 m long, 11.59 m span measured, SR22: 11.68 m) with the origin at the centre of gravity, 1.15 m above the ground when it stands on its wheels.
- Splits the parts that can come off by boxes in the game frame: each wing, the tailplane, the fin, the three gear legs with their wheel fairings, the propeller.
- Cuts the control surfaces out along their real hinge lines (bmesh bisect along the hinge plane and the spanwise ends, so the cuts are clean, about 100 triangles per flap): ailerons, flaps, elevator, rudder, each with its hinge axis exported as a glTF extra (`hinge_axis`) and parented to its wing or tailplane.
- Paints out the registration and the logos (each box filled with the colour around it), keeps the author's signature, renames the materials the game reacts to (`paint`, `glass`, `prop`, `spinner`, `interior`) and compresses with meshopt: 1.03 MB, 59.9k triangles, 36 meshes in 19 parts.
- `tests/aircraft-model.test.ts` checks the result against the flight model: span and length, every part and hinge present, each wheel under its strut at the rest length, each lifting surface inside its part, the propeller hub on the thrust line.

### Fitting the flight model to it

The surfaces of `src/vehicle/aircraft/sr22.json` were placed on the model's planform, measured by casting rays through the wing and tail meshes on a 20 cm grid: wing area 13.4 m2 (SR22: 13.5), mean aerodynamic centre at z = -0.02 m, 4 degrees of dihedral, tailplane 2.52 m2 at 4 m, fin 1.27 m2 at 4.4 m; the wheels at the model's hubs (radius 0.215 and 0.2 m) with the struts at their static compression; the eleven mass boxes laid over the cabin, cowling, tail cone, wings and tail with the centre of mass 2 cm behind the reference point and 15 % of the weight on the nose wheel.

### Cost

With the aircraft close to the camera (chase view on the runway), the first version cost 1 to 2 ms of GPU time: every material two-sided, and the cabin's insides (35k of the 60k triangles: panel, instruments, seats) drawn into the shadow map. Now only the shell casts shadows, the insides are one-sided and hidden beyond 35 m: the aircraft costs about 0.3 ms, the two parked ones (merged into one mesh per material, without instruments) about 0.2 ms. The airfield adds about 30 draw calls (its markings and lights are merged into one mesh per material; the PAPI faces are 8 small meshes).

Not done: a lower-detail model for far away (the aircraft is small on screen beyond 200 m, and its 36 meshes still cost 36 draw calls plus the shadows); spinning wheels (the wheels sit in their fairings, which the import cannot separate cleanly from the tyres).

## 8. Fighter (2026-11-24)

The user asked for a fighter in place of the light aircraft, flying like one. Candidates on Sketchfab, downloadable under CC BY 4.0 and detailed enough to cut into parts:

| Model | Author | Triangles | Likes | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **[General Dynamics F-16D Block 60](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/general-dynamics-f-16d-block-60-ea8edb08d79e4eafa9c794c581f88cf3)** (picked by the user) | MirzaArrafiERV_45 | 61k | 376 | Full cockpit, separate glass; carries stores; tagged as generated with AI |
| MiG-23 MLD | citizensnip | 43k | 1,979 | Most liked; swing wing, harder to cut |
| F/A-18 Hornet | andertan | 58k | 408 | Twin fins, folded-wing parts |
| Dassault Rafale | andertan | 16k | 198 | Light, less detail in the cockpit |

**Import.** The stores (two missiles, the wing tanks and pylons, a stray object below the fuselage) are dropped before anything is measured; the model is oriented nose to +z, scaled to 15.06 m, the origin at the centre of gravity (2.10 m above the ground on its wheels, 30 % of the mean chord). 44k triangles, 1.9 MB after meshopt. `tests/aircraft-model.test.ts` checks the size, the parts and hinges, that nothing of the wings is left on the fuselage (the wingtip lights included, split out of the glass), the wheels on the struts and the surfaces in their parts.

**Flight model.** `src/vehicle/aircraft/f16.json`: six surfaces on the measured planform (27.8 m2 of wing, stabilators 5.9 m2, fin 5.1 m2), a jet engine (76 kN dry, 127 kN with afterburner, spool-up, transonic drag rise, air brake), gear at the model's wheels, nine mass boxes (12,160 kg, 16 % on the nose wheel). Two engine-level changes came with it:

- **Inertia axes.** The arcade controller used `principalInertia()` as if it were about the body's x, y and z. It is in Rapier's principal frame, whose axes come in any order: for the F-16 the roll inertia (19,700 kg m2) came out on x and the yaw inertia (174,000) on z, so the roll loop pushed nine times too hard and the aircraft span up at 94 rad/s. `bodyInertia()` now projects the principal moments on the body axes (`tests/f16.test.ts` checks the roll inertia is the smallest). The SR22's frame was also turned (its roll and yaw moments were swapped, 1.4 times off), harmless at its size.
- **Fly-by-wire in the simulation.** The stabilator alone can pull 20 g at 1,000 km/h. With `fbw` in the config, the simulation flies a g-command law: the stick asks for a load factor (1 g centred, 9 g full back, -3 g full forward), an integrator on the error (its gain falling with the dynamic pressure) moves the stabilator, acting on the g 0.3 s ahead and with some pitch damping, and an angle-of-attack limiter stops it at 25 degrees. Gains picked by sweeping step responses at 90 to 300 m/s: no divergence, a full pull settles about 8 g. The arcade mode keeps its rate controller, with the fighter's rates (250 degrees a second in roll, no wing levelling).

**Measured** (`tests/f16.test.ts`): lift-off in about 320 m with the afterburner and 500 m dry, Mach 0.95 dry and 1.07 with afterburner at sea level, 1.14 at 1,500 m. Rapier caps linear velocity at 400 m/s (with `lengthUnit` 1), which the game keeps.

**In the game.** The F-16 starts on runway 27 (`Fly the F-16`), the two SR22s stay parked on the apron with their own model and boxes. Afterburner flame (two additive cones at the nozzle), engine smoke at the nozzle, wingtip and tail lights moved, the stabilators animated as one, the cockpit interior hidden beyond 35 m, a longer chase camera and a cockpit eye in the canopy, jet sound (rumble, whine, roar), Mach and spool on the instruments, an F-16 silhouette for the damage diagram.
